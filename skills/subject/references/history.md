# History: source work and connected explanations

Start with the [subject guide](../subjects/history.md) to choose the question,
period, and scale. Use this reference when a lesson needs close reading,
conflicting sources, a causal explanation, or a map or series with uncertain
evidence. Choose tools through
[lesson design](../../lesson-design/references/representation-choices.md).

## Build the setting before the argument

Give the learner the people, places, institutions, and earlier events needed
to understand the first question. Use a short dated sequence, then explain why
one event changed the options available at the next. A timeline establishes
order; the explanation must still establish the connection.

Return to the moment before a decision. Identify feasible choices, constraints,
and what the actors could know. Separate long conditions, immediate triggers,
decisions, and consequences. Compare an alternative without treating an
unobserved outcome as fact. This keeps the later result from making the choice
seem inevitable.

## Teach the first source reading

Choose a short legible passage or image with enough context to interpret it.
Name maker, date, audience, genre, purpose, and provenance. Translate unfamiliar
vocabulary while preserving its period meaning. Work through one inference:
what the source directly records, what it suggests, and what it cannot settle.

For example, an authenticated diary can record the writer's fear without
establishing the number of people at an event. Explain why a biased source may
still answer a narrower question. Pair the source with a caption or annotation
that points to the evidence used; a link alone does not teach the reading.

## Compare records before combining their claims

Choose records whose differences matter to the question: a public speech and
a private letter, a census and a personal account, or a rule and an enforcement
record. Keep their dates and audiences visible. Use an evidence table to compare
claims, access, incentives, and corroboration.

Explain disagreements before trying to resolve them. They may reflect different
moments, definitions, knowledge, or purposes. More surviving records do not
necessarily represent more people. Identify whose experience is missing and
limit the conclusion accordingly. A new source should change a specific claim,
not merely add another quotation.

## Connect maps and numbers to how records were made

For a map, state the date, scale, boundary source, and uncertain or reconstructed
features. Compare regions only after checking their local chronologies. Motion
can show a documented route or changing boundary; label a reconstruction and
keep its evidence available beside it.

For prices, wages, migration, or population, inspect units, coverage, collection
rules, and missing data before plotting. Explain whether a change may reflect
record keeping rather than the process being studied. Connect the pattern to
documents and institutions. An economic model can suggest a mechanism but
cannot supply historical actors' unrecorded intentions.

## Build and revise an interpretation

Develop a bounded claim, work through its evidence, and consider the strongest
competing account. Distinguish disagreement about records from disagreement
about concepts, scale, or inference. Show how a new record would narrow or
revise the claim. Do not turn “both sides” into a substitute for weighing support.

A useful final task changes the source, period, or social group and asks the
learner to reconsider the explanation. Provide enough context to make that
comparison possible. Recall of dates alone does not establish source judgment.

## Select the source set

Use a scholarly overview for orientation and inspect the primary items used
for the actual claim. The catalog's `openstax-world-history-1` covers an early
world-history route, not every period or region. `loc-source-analysis` is a
source-analysis lead. Inspect the current section or item before assigning it;
prior access notes do not establish present access or suitability.

For a source packet, keep excerpts, captions, provenance, and comparison
questions together. Audio and video need the same source criticism as written
records. A generated image can illustrate a stated reconstruction; it cannot
serve as primary evidence. Keep Elias's voice focused on what the evidence
allows the learner to say.
