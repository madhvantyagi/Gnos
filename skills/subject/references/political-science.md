# Political science: institutions, comparison, and evidence

Start with the [subject guide](../subjects/political-science.md). Use this
reference when a lesson needs a worked institutional process, an election
calculation, or a comparison of explanations. Choose tools through
[lesson design](../../lesson-design/references/representation-choices.md).

## Follow one decision through an institution

Name the jurisdiction, period, actors, and decision. Inspect the actual rule
before drawing proposal, approval, veto, implementation, review, or appeal.
Trace one case through the procedure and explain what each actor can change.
Then compare the formal path with documented practice. Mark discretion and
noncompliance where the evidence supports them.

Use a diagram to show authority and a timeline to show sequence. Give arrows
one meaning: authority, information, money, or order. Narrated motion can
follow one case, but the complete path should also be inspectable at rest.
Change one veto or review condition and explain which steps would differ.

## Separate an election rule from voter behavior

Begin with explicit vote totals and work the allocation rule by hand. Carry
the same totals into a table or visual. Change a threshold, district boundary,
or allocation method and recalculate. A control is useful when several changes
are worth comparing.

Distinguish what follows mechanically from the rule from what requires evidence
about turnout, strategic voting, parties, or campaigning. Inspect the official
rule for the relevant election and date, then the data definitions. Label toy
votes as hypothetical. Do not imply that changing the rule would leave actual
voter behavior unchanged.

## Make bargaining assumptions explicit

Name actors, resources or seats, feasible agreements, and a winning threshold.
Start with a small case and enumerate possible coalitions before discussing a
general explanation. A network can show compatibility; weighted blocks can
show seats. Spatial distance needs a declared meaning.

Change one actor's position or available information and compare the feasible
agreements. Distinguish an agreement permitted by the model from one observed
in records. Research the bargaining assumptions and the case evidence separately.

## Trace implementation before judging outcomes

Connect authorization, funding, administration, frontline decisions, take-up,
and measured outcomes. Explain where the intended policy can differ from the
treatment people actually receive. A process diagram can locate that gap;
data or records must establish whether it occurred.

Read the policy text, implementation records, and evaluation methods. Keep
adoption, delivery, and effect as separate claims. Compare groups or periods
only after checking eligibility, selection, timing, and the proposed mechanism.
Use a simulation to explore a stated assumption, not to establish an effect.

## Compare explanations and arguments

For an empirical dispute, place each explanation beside the observation that
would distinguish it from the others. In international relations, separate
capability, preference, information, commitment, and action. A dated map or
timeline provides context; it does not settle a motive.

For political theory, identify the claim, normative premises, and strongest
objection. Work a concrete case that puts a premise under pressure. Keep a
source excerpt beside the argument map so paraphrase does not replace the
author's position. Do not give unsupported accounts equal weight merely to
make a symmetrical comparison.

## Inspect measurement and sources

For surveys, retain question wording, field dates, sampling, weights, and
nonresponse information. Show population, sample, measure, and uncertainty as
different things. For causal work, mark assumptions and unobserved variables.
Preserve contested categories as the source defines them and explain their
limits before aggregating groups.

Use original theory texts, dated legal and institutional records, official
election data, and original research for their respective claims. Verify
current rules and officeholders when they matter. A history or mathematics
bridge should return to the same political question and comparison.
