# Political-science depth and visual patterns

Read this reference when a political-science lesson needs a substantial visual,
comparison design, source route, or worked institutional example. The concise
route remains in [the subject guide](../subjects/political-science.md).

Use the guide's representation profile first, then apply
`skills/course-design/references/representation-choices.md`. This reference
defines political content and visual grammar; it does not order media by
itself.

## Frame the claim first

Name the jurisdiction, period, actors, formal rules, observed practice, and
comparison. Keep empirical mechanisms separate from normative premises. A
visual can clarify who can act, block, implement, or benefit; it cannot establish
that the formal rule was enforced or that the proposed mechanism caused an
outcome.

## Pinepaper patterns

Read [the Pinepaper workflow](pinepaper.md) before using the MCP tools.

### Institutional process

Draw actors and decision points according to the actual formal procedure. Use
distinct paths for proposal, approval, veto, review, implementation, and
appeal. Animate one hypothetical case through the process, then compare the
formal path with documented practice. Mark where discretion or enforcement
enters.

### Elections and representation

Begin from explicit hypothetical vote totals. Show the conversion rule from
votes to seats or offices, then animate one changed threshold, district rule,
or allocation method. Separate outcomes implied by the rule from behavioral
responses that require evidence.

### Coalitions and bargaining

Use a network or weighted blocks to show actors, seats or resources, compatible
positions, and winning thresholds. Let one actor or payoff change and recompute
the viable coalitions. Spatial closeness should represent a declared dimension,
not a vague claim that actors are “similar.”

### Policy implementation

Trace authorization, funding, administration, frontline discretion, uptake,
and measured outcome. Keep the stated policy, implemented treatment, and
observed result distinct. Animate breakdowns or feedback only when the evidence
or model supports those links.

### International relations and conflict

Use timelines, actor networks, or maps only for sourced geography, sequence,
alliances, trade, or communication. Distinguish capability, preference,
information, commitment, and observed action. Motion should not turn one
theoretical mechanism into settled history.

### Political behavior and causal evidence

Show population, sample, measurement, treatment or exposure, comparison, and
uncertainty as separate objects. For surveys, preserve question wording and
field dates. For causal diagrams, mark assumptions and unobserved variables;
do not animate an arrow as proof of causation.

## Design and verification

- Give arrows one declared meaning: authority, information, resources,
  sequence, or hypothesized causal influence.
- Label formal rules and observed behavior differently.
- Date institutional claims and data; retain source credits in the artifact.
- Keep groups and contested categories faithful to the source instead of
  inventing neutral-looking aggregates.
- After rendering, ask what would change under one rule, actor, or information
  condition. The learner should identify which result follows mechanically and
  which requires behavioral evidence.

## Sources and handoffs

Use constitutional and legislative texts, official election data, administrative
records, original research, and inspected theory texts. Verify current laws and
officeholders. History owns chronology and source context; economics supports
incentives; psychology supports behavior; mathematics supports measurement.
Political science retains ownership of institutions, comparison, and political
concepts.
